A few weeks ago I posted that a "bundled" word representation (text + a visual grounding + a physical grounding) could separate word senses that text alone merges: "crane" the bird from "crane" the machine. The probe passed. So I trained the actual model to see if the separation would help it. It didn't. Here's the honest result and what it taught me.

The setup: two identical small language models, matched compute. One starts from the grounded bundle. One starts from random. Then a fair test, 105 sense-disambiguation pairs, does the model prefer the sense-correct next word?

Result: the grounded model scored 0.79, the random baseline 0.83. The grounding lost by ~4 points, and the gap isn't even statistically meaningful.

The interesting part is why. I checked how correlated the two trained models were, and they'd converged to almost the same function (0.96). Over billions of training tokens, the model simply overwrote the grounded starting point and learned disambiguation from scratch, the same way the baseline did. The head start didn't survive contact with training.

Which reframes the whole bet. Injecting meaning up front can only help if the model would otherwise struggle to learn it. At full training scale, it doesn't struggle, so a warm start is at best neutral (and here, slightly negative, from two fixable wiring issues I found in the post-mortem). I ran the experiment in exactly the regime where the idea was designed NOT to matter.

The real claim was always about efficiency: a smaller model, or less data. That test, the same comparison at a fraction of the compute, is running now. If grounding wins there and ties at full scale, the thesis holds, it's a data-efficiency lever, not a ceiling-raiser. If it loses there too, the "bake it into the embedding" approach is dead and I move the grounding elsewhere.

Two things I keep relearning:

A passing probe is not a passing model. The representation separating senses and the representation helping a trained model are different claims, and it's tempting to treat the first as evidence for the second. It isn't.

A negative result with a clean diagnosis is worth as much as a win. I now know precisely why it failed and what regime to test next, which is more useful than a fluke pass would have been.
